\section{The Architecture of Persistence}
\label{sec:architecture_of_persistence}
Before a system can grow or adapt, it must preserve its identity and survive. We organize the conditions for persistence into three core constraints: Finiteness, Conservation, and Causality. Because each requirement needs a way to be established and a way to be protected from disruption, this yields six functional pillars of persistence. This section defines those pillars, examines the failure modes they address, and shows how familiar  capabilities can be composed from them.

\subsection{The Structural Constraints}
\label{subsec:existential_constraints}
% To exist something must fulfill 3 constraints
Any system persisting through time is bounded in extent, carries distinguishable content, and evolves through an ordered sequence of states. Absent any one, it is indistinguishable from its environment, from noise, or from something static. Formally, these correspond to: \textbf{Finiteness} (bounded resources against divergence), \textbf{Conservation} (preserved information against dissipation), and \textbf{Causality} (well-defined temporal evolution against ambiguity).


% With the 3 we can say something exists, but need to survive too
To persist, each constraint must survive its characteristic perturbation. Each therefore demands not only \textbf{provision} (establishing the means to satisfy the constraint), but also \textbf{defense} (protecting that means from the characteristic failure mode) yielding the six functional pillars.

A \emph{pillar} is a functionally distinct condition that must be materially or informationally instantiated for persistence. Pillars need not correspond one-to-one with components: a single mechanism may instantiate several pillars, and one pillar may be distributed across many mechanisms.

These pillars operate upon a medium that imposes limits (bandwidth, latency, noise floor) referred to as a \textbf{Substrate}.


\subsection{The Six Pillars}
\label{subsec:pillars}

For each pillar, we define it and connect it to established mathematical results that provide a basis for formal analysis.

\begin{enumerate}[label={}, leftmargin=0pt]
% Finiteness
    \item \textbf{Capacity ($CAP$):}
    The resources and infrastructure available to maintain the system's organization and carry out its operations. Capacity sets how much the system can store, process, and sustain.
    \textit{Formal Connection (The Bekenstein Bound\cite{bekenstein_universal_1981}):} For physical systems within its scope, Bekenstein's bound limits information storage in terms of energy and spatial extent. It provides a concrete realization of Capacity: the information a system can retain is constrained by its physical resources.

    \item \textbf{Margin ($MAR$):}
    The reserves and operating tolerances that allow the system to absorb disturbances without leaving its viable range.
    \textit{Formal Connection (Nyquist-Bode Stability Margins\cite{nyquist_regeneration_1932} and Kingman's Formula\cite{kingman_single_1961}):} In linear control, gain and phase margins quantify how much loop gain or phase can change before stability is lost. Similarly, Kingman's heavy-traffic approximation shows that, with nonzero arrival or service-time variability, mean waiting time grows without bound as queueing utilization approaches unity. Both results illustrate the role of operating margins in sustaining performance under uncertainty.

% Conservation
    \item \textbf{Map ($MAP$):}
    The retained organization or relational structure that defines the system and preserves distinctions relevant to its continued operation. In adaptive systems, Map may be instantiated as an internal predictive model; in passive systems, it may be embodied directly in physical organization.
    \textit{Formal Connection (The Good-Regulator Theorem \cite{conant_every_1970}):} Conant and Ashby's Good-Regulator theorem establishes that, under its assumptions, the simplest optimal regulator must embody a model of the system it regulates.

    \item \textbf{Protocol ($PRO$):}
    The mechanisms through which components exchange information and coordinate state transitions. Protocol supports internal coherence by preserving relevant distinctions across the system's interfaces.
    \textit{Formal Connection (Shannon's Source-Channel Separation and Channel Capacity \cite{shannon_mathematical_1948}):} Shannon's coding results characterize conditions for reliable communication, providing a realization of the Protocol.

% Causality
    \item \textbf{Toll ($TOL$):}
    The resource expenditure and time required to realize state transitions.
    \textit{Formal Connection (Landauer's Principle and the Margolus--Levitin Limit\cite{landauer_irreversibility_1961,margolus_maximum_1998}):} Under standard isothermal erasure conditions, resetting one unbiased bit requires heat dissipation of at least $k_BT\ln 2$. The Margolus--Levitin limit bounds the time required for a quantum system to evolve to an orthogonal state at a specified mean energy above its ground state. These results formalize energetic and temporal constraints on state transitions.

    \item \textbf{Governor ($GOV$):}
    The active or passive constraints that restrict which transitions the system can undergo, suppressing, redirecting, or terminating those that threaten its viable operation.
    \textit{Formal Connection (Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety\cite{ashby_introduction_1956}):} Ashby's law bounds the regulatory variety required to restrict the effects of disturbances to an acceptable range of outcomes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Necessity: Counterfactual Failure Modes}
\label{subsec:pillars_necessity}
The necessity of the six pillars follows by analyzing the \textbf{Counterfactual Failure Mode} of a system lacking exactly one pillar. In every case, the system's viability collapses, limiting its expected lifetime $\tau$ to the onset of its characteristic perturbation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No Capacity (Starvation):} Without baseline operational infrastructure, the system cannot acquire, process, or export resources. Once existing reserves are spent, any perturbation is fatal; $\tau \to 0$ via rapid exhaustion.

    \item \textbf{No Margin (Fragility):} Operating at theoretical criticality leaves zero tolerance for environmental variance. Any non-zero perturbation exceeds the system's structural limits immediately, so the expected time to a fatal shock is finite; $\tau \to 0$ by random shock.

   \item \textbf{No Map (Dissolution):} Without an internal model, the system has no reference for coherence, and no boundary to maintain against its environment; $\tau \to 0$ as the system--environment boundary dissolves. 

    \item \textbf{No Protocol (Decoherence):} Components act on statistically independent, outdated states, with no mechanism to reconcile them; $\tau \to 0$ by organizational fragmentation.

    \item \textbf{No Toll (Zeno Collapse):} A causal sequence requires irreducibly discrete state transitions. Without an irreducible toll per operation, the sequence can be infinitely subdivided; no individual step remains distinguishable as a cause of the next, and the ordering required for well-defined evolution dissolves. $\tau \to 0$ by causal decoherence.
    
    \item \textbf{No Governor (Divergence):} Unconstrained positive feedback drives resource consumption toward unbounded growth, exhausting the substrate or diverging past any recoverable bound; $\tau \to 0$ by unbounded runaway.
\end{itemize}
    
Since the removal of any component results in $\tau \to 0$, every pillar is necessary.


\subsection{Completeness: Compositional Closure}
\label{subsec:pillars_sufficiency}
We conjecture that the six pillars are sufficient to account for the functional requirements of persistence: no additional independent role is needed. We do not yet have a general proof. Every candidate requirement we have examined can be expressed as a composition of the six pillars, and we have identified no counterexample to date.

The following examples illustrate these compositions without enumerating every supporting role. Memory is Map content provisioned against temporal variance by Margin. Prediction is Map operation on incoming signal via Protocol. Repair is Governor-triggered re-provisioning within the Margin reserve. Replication is Protocol-mediated write of Map onto fresh Capacity. Feedforward networks, state observers, and adaptive controllers can likewise be described through combinations of the six roles, without requiring an additional primitive in these descriptions.


\subsection{The Cost of Persistence}
\label{subsec:cost_of_persistence}
A system consumes resources by operating the six pillars. Expressed in common units, these costs provide a basis for comparing systems and, when related to available resources and failure mechanisms, their lifetimes.

To distinguish baseline structural costs from task-related expenditure, we evaluate baseline structural costs in a \emph{quiescent steady state} (QSS), taken here as stationary operation at zero task flux. Internal housekeeping and supporting resource exchanges may continue.

Protocol and Governor incur operating costs but may reduce expenditure elsewhere. Relative to a specified uncoordinated baseline, their net contribution can be negative when the savings exceed their operating costs. Let $Z_i$ denote the cost contribution allocated to pillar $i$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cost_of_persistence}
    \bar{Z}_{\mathrm{net}} = Z_{\mathrm{CAP}} + Z_{\mathrm{MAP}} + Z_{\mathrm{TOL}} + Z_{\mathrm{MAR}} + Z_{\mathrm{PRO}} + Z_{\mathrm{GOV}}
\end{equation}

Because the pillars occupy orthogonal domains at zero flux, any correlation between them is already priced into the individual coordination terms $Z_{\mathrm{PRO}}$ and $Z_{\mathrm{GOV}}$; no additional cross-term is needed to capture coupling between pillars within this baseline. Coupling outside QSS introduces non-linear contention, which we formalize in \cref{sec:open_systems} on open systems.

\subsection{Relation to Existing Disciplines}
\label{subsec:relations}
Existing disciplines encounter the six functional pillars in the systems they study. While these disciplines also investigate architecture, they primarily address domain-specific problems. PM's contribution is identifying the common underlying organization, regardless of substrate and domain. This gives comparisons across disciplines a shared framework whose implications are explored in \cref{sec:universality_mapping}.

\subsubsection{Relation to Information Theory}
\label{subsec:information_theory}
Information theory quantifies how representation, encoding, and noise constrain communication across physical mediums. In doing so, it relies on structural primitives that map directly to the six pillars: channel capacity (Capacity), codebook topologies (Map), framing and handshaking routines (Protocol), power constraints (Governor), processing overhead (Toll), and link margins (Margin). These problems are analyzed under specified source, channel, and resource assumptions.

\subsubsection{Relation to Thermodynamics}
\label{subsec:thermodynamics}
Thermodynamics describes how physical systems evolve under energy exchange and thermal fluctuations. It relies on physical primitives that correspond to the six pillars: extensive state variables and phase space volume (Capacity), metastable energy landscapes (Map), kinetic transition pathways (Protocol), conservation laws (Governor), irreversible entropy production (Toll), and free-energy activation barriers (Margin). These correspondences draw on thermodynamics, statistical mechanics, and kinetics, each with its own assumptions about states, exchanges, and transition mechanisms.

\subsubsection{Relation to Control Theory} 
\label{subsec:control_theory}
Classical control theory emerged from engineering physical servomechanisms: radar mounts, autopilots, and chemical plants whose historical context was optimizing systems already built, powered, and staffed. Its four given components (the plant, the sensor, the controller, and the actuator) are convergent evidence for four of the six pillars: Plant/Actuator (Capacity), Reference Signal/Setpoint (Map), Sensor/Feedback Path (Protocol), Controller/Limiter (Governor). The remaining two pillars are rarely canonized alongside the first four as they are not physical components, but describe how the four components behave under time and stress. Time delays, bandwidth limits, and processing latency (Toll) were minimized as engineering overhead and gain/phase margins (Margin) were tuned for stability. Control theory asks how to regulate an existing system and the classical four-component loop is a physical instantiation of the six functional roles.
