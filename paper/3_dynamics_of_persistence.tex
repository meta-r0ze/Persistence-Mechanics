\section{The Dynamics of Persistence}
\label{sec:dynamics_of_persistence}
The static architecture of persistence necessitates a minimal dynamical description. A system that persists maintains structural coherence against entropic erosion; therefore it must export internal entropy to its environment at a rate that balances what it produces. We construct the measure of that dissipation from the three irreducible primitives that any dynamical law governing persistent systems must presuppose, prove their necessity, and integrate them into a cumulative Entropic Action that closes the loop back to the six-pillar architecture.


% Define the requirements for dynamics of persistence and define the Quiescent Steady State.
\subsection{The Cost of State Transitions}
\label{subsec:min_entropy}
While a system's architecture provides the capability to persist, the operation of those pillars determines how long it actually persists. Any state transition does work, and all work generates entropy; left unconstrained, a system's own activity can result in its rapid dissolution.

Following Prigogine\cite{prigogine_introduction_1961}, persistence therefore requires two behaviors: internal entropy production must be exported to the environment fast enough that the system's own entropy never rises, and that production rate itself settles to the smallest value consistent with holding steady state ($\delta\sigma = 0$), below this floor a system is not yet stable, and above it, every transition is spending more than persistence strictly requires.

We quantify this at its limiting case, \textbf{QSS}, $Z_{\mathrm{net}}$ is constant, and the entropic balance sheet (\cref{subsec:entropic_balance_sheet}) is strictly additive. The dynamic evolution of open systems is left to future work.


\subsection{The Dissipation Density}
\label{subsec:dissipation_density}
Prigogine's Minimum Entropy Production theorem establishes \textit{that} a persistent system must minimize its entropy production rate, but not \textit{what} defines that rate. We derive the minimum dynamical description by identifying the irreducible primitives that any state transition law must presuppose.

When the static six-pillar architecture moves through time, any state transition (a local dissipative event) can be described along three axes inherent to the concept of change itself: what changes (a structured payload), what permits the change to occur (an energetic toll), and how often change occurs (a temporal frequency). These are
not assigned to individual pillars, but are properties of the transition itself.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A structured payload (The Subject):} A persistent system maintains a specific configuration rather than a statistical ensemble. Its local state is quantified by the structural complexity $\mathbf{K}$: the Kolmogorov complexity of the local configuration relative to the substrate's reference encoding, expressed in substrate-natural units of elementary logical operations per local state (up to the invariant additive constant of the substrate's instruction set).\footnote{Uncomputability does not preclude operational use: the algorithmic-information-theory literature routinely substitutes computable proxies: Minimum Description Length, Lempel-Ziv complexity, normalized compression distance, as finite-sample estimators of $\mathbf{K}$, a practice this paper's baseline derivation does not require but that applied instantiations can draw on directly.} \textit{Convergent Evidence (Kolmogorov Complexity \cite{kolmogorov_three_1968}):} The algorithmic information content of a state is the irreducible payload carried by any transition.

    \item \textbf{An irreducible toll (The Cost):} Every transition exacts a cost per elementary logical operation, $\xi$, expressed as a dimensionless number in fundamental units. \textit{Convergent Evidence (Landauer's Principle \cite{landauer_irreversibility_1961}):} Every irreversible logical operation carries a thermodynamic cost of at least $k_B T \ln 2$.

    \item \textbf{A coordinated rate (The Frequency):} State updates must occur at a transition flux $f$, a rate density of state updates per unit substrate measure. \textit{Convergent Evidence (The Nyquist--Shannon Sampling Theorem \cite{shannon_communication_1949}):} A signal can only be reconstructed without loss if sampled at a rate exceeding its bandwidth; below that rate, information is irrecoverably lost to aliasing. $f$ is the substrate-level analogue of this minimum sampling rate, required to track the Map against noise.
\end{enumerate}

The pillars operate on orthogonal domains (\cref{subsec:entropic_balance_sheet}) in the QSS; $\mathbf{K}$, $\xi$, and $f$ inherit this independence, each drawing on a distinct domain: algorithmic description, informational toll, and temporal rate, respectively. 

Because a local dissipation metric must yield a uniform density of elementary transitions per unit substrate measure, its functional form is dimensionally constrained, these brackets track substrate counting units (operations, states, actions), not physical mass-length-time dimensions, the same sense in which a bit count or a
radian is dimensionless yet still carried explicitly through a derivation. With $\mathbf{K}$ in [operations / local state], $\xi$ in [action / operation], and $f$ in [states / (substrate measure $\cdot$ time)], their strictly linear product yields $\mathcal{D}$ in [action / (substrate measure $\cdot$ time)].

The local \textbf{Dissipation Density} is therefore:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dissipation_density}
    \mathcal{D} = \xi \cdot \mathbf{K} \cdot f
\end{equation}


\subsection{Necessity: Counterfactual Failure Modes}
\label{subsec:action_necessity}

The necessity of $(\xi, \mathbf{K}, f)$ follows by the same counterfactual method used for the pillars (\cref{subsec:pillars_necessity}): removing any factor drives lifetime to zero, as the local manifestation of its corresponding pillar failure mode.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No $\xi$ (Zeno Collapse):} Transitions become thermodynamically free, so the system attempts infinitely many in finite time, collapsing the causal sequence. $\tau \to 0$, the local form of No-Toll.
    
    \item \textbf{No $\mathbf{K}$ (Dissolution):} No distinguishable local states remain; random activation maximizes uncertainty and the system--environment boundary dissolves. $\tau \to 0$, the local form of No-Map.
    
    \item \textbf{No $f$ (Decoherence/Divergence):} The system freezes into a static configuration, decoupled from its own history and unable to mount a defensive response. $\tau \to 0$, the local form of No-Protocol/No-Governor.
\end{itemize}

Since the removal of any factor results in termination, the set $(\xi, \mathbf{K}, f)$ is necessary.

\subsection{Necessity of the Product Form}
\label{subsec:product_form_necessity}
The product form, the geometric mean and other symmetric functions all vanishes when any factor reaches zero. What the product form uniquely satisfies is extensivity: doubling any one factor while holding the other two fixed must double the local dissipation, since twice the transition rate at identical cost and complexity is twice the dissipation, not a fractional multiple. Among functions vanishing correctly on any factor's absence, only the linear product $\mathcal{D} = \xi \cdot \mathbf{K} \cdot f$ is extensive in each factor independently; fractional-power forms such as $(\xi \cdot \mathbf{K} \cdot f)^{1/3}$ satisfy vanishing but not extensivity, and are excluded on that ground.

\subsection{Sufficiency: No Fourth Factor}
\label{subsec:action_sufficiency}
Given the multiplicative form, a missing factor would drive $\mathcal{D}$ to zero rather than merely reduce it, leaving no independent dimension for a fourth factor to occupy. Any property proposed as a fourth factor, on inspection, either reduces to a combination of these three or describes something about the environment rather than the system itself.

\subsection{The Complexity Trade-off}
\label{subsec:complexity_tradeoff}
The product form of the Dissipation Density implies a finite-budget competition among the three factors.

\begin{proposition}[The Complexity Trade-off]
For any system in QSS, the substrate's finite Capacity imposes a dissipation ceiling $\mathcal{D}_{\text{max}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \xi \cdot \mathbf{K} \cdot f \le \mathcal{D}_{\text{max}}
\end{equation}
No system can simultaneously maximize structural complexity, operational speed, and processing efficiency: increasing any one factor requires a proportional decrease in the product of the other two.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Exceeding $\mathcal{D}_{\max}$ at any point violates the stationary condition $\delta\sigma = 0$ (\cref{subsec:min_entropy}); substituting the unique product form (\cref{eq:dissipation_density}) into this ceiling yields the inequality directly.
\end{proof}

A biological cell illustrates the binding constraint: ATP cost per operation ($\xi$), proteome complexity ($\mathbf{K}$), and metabolic turnover ($f$) together cannot exceed the thermodynamic ceiling of the cellular volume. A cell cannot maximize proteome complexity and reaction frequency at once without $\mathcal{D}$ diverging.

\textit{Convergent Evidence:} As established for Capacity (\cref{subsec:pillars}), the Margolus-Levitin limit and Bekenstein bound jointly cap the achievable volume in $(\xi, \mathbf{K}, f)$-space, one bounding $f$ for any finite $\xi$, the other bounding $\mathbf{K}$.


\subsection{The Entropic Action}
\label{subsec:entropic_action}
Having established the conditions for structural survival, we need a way to compare surviving systems by lifetime. A system's viability cannot be judged pointwise: no single pillar sustains a transition alone, so no single local event determines survival either, what accumulates across the whole system is what matters. We build that accumulated quantity in two steps: first collapsing the system's spatial extent into one number at each instant, then accumulating that number over its lifetime.

$\mathcal{D}$'s multiplicative, local form and $Z_{\mathrm{net}}$'s additive, global one (\cref{subsec:entropic_balance_sheet}) are reconciled by integrating over the system's spatial footprint $\Sigma(t)$: many local multiplicative events, summed, become one global additive quantity at each instant:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:impedance_density}
    Z_{\mathrm{net}}(t) = \int_{\Sigma(t)} \mathcal{D} \, d\mu
\end{equation}
\noindent with $\mathrm{d}\mu$ the counting measure on the discrete substrate state space, a sum over discrete points, not a continuous integral.

With the impedance $Z_{\mathrm{net}}(t)$ known to be the system's total instantaneous dissipation, the cumulative \textbf{Entropic Action} is the time integral of that impedance over the system's lifetime:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:entropic_action}
    S_\Phi = \int_0^{\tau} Z_{\mathrm{net}}(t) \, dt
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Physical Meaning of the Entropic Action}
Because $\mathcal{D}$ counts elementary state transitions per unit substrate measure, $S_\Phi$ is a pure, dimensionless count of irreversible transitions, not yet an energy. When the dimensionless action $S_\Phi$ is projected onto a thermal physical substrate, Landauer's principle supplies the thermodynamic scale $\xi_0 = k_B T \ln 2$, yielding the physical dissipation analogue $E_{\mathrm{diss}} = \xi_0 S_\Phi$. On athermal substrates, $S_\Phi$ remains well-defined; only the conversion factor to macroscopic energy changes.

\subsubsection{Necessity of the Architecture for the Action}
\label{subsec:action_requires_architecture}
Minimization of $S_\Phi$ is only possible for systems implementing the complete six-pillar architecture; the Entropic Action is not defined independently of it.

\begin{proof}
Minimizing $S_\Phi$ requires $\mathcal{D}$ strictly positive and finite, hence $\xi, \mathbf{K}, f > 0$ (\cref{eq:dissipation_density}). By the pillar-to-factor dependencies established in \cref{subsec:dissipation_density}, this requires all six pillars simultaneously: Toll and Capacity sustain $\xi > 0$; Map sustains $\mathbf{K} > 0$; Protocol, Governor, and Margin jointly sustain $f > 0$. Removing any one pillar collapses the corresponding factor to zero (reproducing the failure modes of \cref{subsec:action_necessity}) so any trajectory with $\delta S_\Phi = 0$ that maintains structural coherence necessarily encodes the complete architecture.
\end{proof}

The Entropic Action is therefore not defined independently of the architecture; the two are one closed claim.

\subsection{The Selection Principle}
\label{subsec:selection_principle}

The Entropic Action defines \textit{what} a system must minimize; we now state the filter that determines \textit{how} minimization manifests in observation, reserving \textit{which path} a surviving system follows for \cref{sec:minimum_action}. Configurations do not \emph{seek} minimization; the principle is eliminative. What fails to minimize leaves no detectable signal, and what persists is the minimal-action remainder:

\begin{samepage}
\begin{mdframed}[linewidth=1pt,linecolor=black,backgroundcolor=gray!10]
    \noindent The \textbf{Selection Principle}: Among all configurations capable of encoding a given structural complexity, only those possessing the minimal possible \textbf{Entropic Action} ($S_\Phi$) remain observable.
\end{mdframed}
\end{samepage}

Because $S_\Phi$ counts elementary transitions, it is the cumulative ledger of a system's dissipation history. The substrate does not optimize this ledger; it erases ledgers that fail to balance.
