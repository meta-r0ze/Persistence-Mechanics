\section{Introduction}
From biological organisms to engineered feedback loops, complex systems science describes how structures maintain coherence against entropic disturbances. These structures share a defining property: they \textit{persist} against the current that erases distinction. Control theory describes how systems regulate against fluctuations, taking the regulation target (setpoint) as an externally specified boundary condition. Information theory quantifies the cost of information processing, taking the processor as given. Thermodynamics establishes the arrow of time, treating entropy as a property of states. These three fields developed independently to solve distinct engineering problems: thermodynamics from heat engines, information theory from telecommunication, and control theory from servomechanisms. Each optimizes systems already embedded in a supportive environment.

Here, we address the preceding gap: the minimal architecture required before optimization or dynamics can even be written. Grounding this architecture in algorithmic information theory, IE functions as an abstraction layer beneath thermodynamics and control theory, which emerge as substrate-specific instantiations of the same invariant.

Previous complex systems research has converged on pieces of this puzzle from multiple directions. Prigogine established that persistent structures occupy a minimum-entropy-production attractor\cite{prigogine_introduction_1961}; England showed how external drive selects for structural configurations in non-equilibrium steady states\cite{england_dissipative_2015}; and cybernetics has catalogued the organizational patterns, such as requisite variety and good regulation, that correlate with survival\cite{ashby_introduction_1956,conant_every_1970}. Individual domains have independently named the specific components that perform these same functions, without recognizing them as instances of one shared architecture. What these frameworks share is that they treat the persistent system, or its named components, as given: they describe how a structure maintains itself, adapts, or is selected, but do not formally define the minimal architecture required before any of those dynamics can begin.

IE consists of two universal components. First, the \textbf{Architecture of Persistence} (\cref{sec:architecture_of_persistence}): six substrate-independent pillars extracted from the logical requirements of Finiteness, Conservation, and Causality. This architecture specifies what must exist, not how it behaves under transition. Second, the \textbf{Dynamics of Persistence}(\cref{sec:dynamics_of_persistence}): the universal behavior governing how any persistent system dissipates, accumulates entropic action, and undergoes selection. We derive the dissipation density $\mathcal{D} = \xi \cdot \mathbf{K} \cdot f$ as the minimum dynamical description any state transition law must presuppose.

\Cref{sec:least_action} demonstrates that the Principle of Least Action emerges as the boundary of survivorship: trajectories that do not minimize their Entropic Action undergo exponential suppression, leaving only the least-action remainder as observable history. \Cref{sec:universality_mapping} then illustrates the architecture's mapping across biological, ecological, and computational substrates.
