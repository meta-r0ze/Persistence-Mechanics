\section{Introduction}
From biological organisms to engineered feedback loops, a central problem in complex-systems science is how systems retain recognizable organization despite disturbance, turnover, and decay
\cite{schrodinger_what_1992}. Several mature disciplines address particular problems faced by such systems: control theory characterizes regulation and stability; information theory characterizes
representation and communication under noise; and thermodynamics constrains energy conversion and dissipation. Each typically begins with a system boundary, state space, and relevant mechanisms already
specified. This leaves a prior cross-domain question: what minimal architecture must any system instantiate simply to remain identifiable through time?

Previous complex-systems research has explored complementary aspects of persistence. Prigogine characterized minimum entropy production in stationary states under linear near-equilibrium conditions \cite{prigogine_introduction_1961}; Friston related adaptive self-maintenance to variational free-energy minimization through an internal model \cite{friston_free-energy_2010}; England examined how external driving can favor particular configurations in nonequilibrium systems \cite{england_dissipative_2015}; and cybernetics established organizational constraints on effective regulation, including requisite variety and the Good-Regulator theorem \cite{ashby_introduction_1956,conant_every_1970}. These approaches characterize complementary aspects of persistence. Comparing them across domains requires a common criterion for what it means for a system to persist.

We say that a system \emph{persists} over an observation interval when, relative to a specified system boundary and class of disturbances, it retains the organization by which it is identified and remains within its viable operating conditions. Persistence does not require an unchanging microscopic state: components may turn over, resources may flow, and internal states may change, provided the system's defining organization remains intact.

Persistence Mechanics addresses three questions: what must be instantiated for persistence, how the resulting architecture operates through time, and how its cumulative costs affect which histories remain
observable.

\Cref{sec:architecture_of_persistence} derives the \textbf{Architecture of Persistence}: six substrate-independent pillars derived from the logical requirements of Finiteness, Conservation, and Causality. \Cref{sec:dynamics_of_persistence} develops the \textbf{Dynamics of Persistence}, identifying the transition toll $c$, structural complexity $\mathbf{K}$, and coordinated transition flux $f$. \Cref{sec:minimum_action} derives the exponential weighting of histories by cumulative Operational Action and states the conditions under which surviving histories concentrate on minimum-action trajectories. \Cref{sec:open_systems} extends the additive baseline to systems with external flux and shared-resource contention. \Cref{sec:universality_mapping} illustrates how the six pillars are instantiated across biological, ecological, and computational substrates.
