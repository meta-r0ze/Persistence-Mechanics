\section{The Architecture of Persistence}
\label{sec:architecture_of_persistence}
Before a system can regulate, compute, or evolve, it must remain within the conditions that preserve its identity. We organize those conditions along three dimensions: bounded resources, retention of organization, and ordered evolution. Distinguishing the mechanisms that establish these conditions from those that protect them motivates six functional pillars. This section defines those pillars, examines the failure modes they address, and describes how familiar maintenance capabilities can be composed from them.

\subsection{The Structural Constraints}
\label{subsec:existential_constraints}
% To exist something must fulfill 3 constraints
Any process persisting through time is bounded in extent, carries distinguishable content, and evolves through an ordered sequence of states. Absent any one, it is indistinguishable from its environment, from noise, or from something static. Formally, these correspond to: \textbf{Finiteness} (bounded resources against divergence), \textbf{Conservation} (preserved information against dissipation), and \textbf{Causality} (well-defined temporal evolution against ambiguity).

% With the 3 we can say something exists, but need to survive too
Existence, however, is transient. To persist, each constraint must survive its characteristic perturbation. Each therefore demands \textbf{provision} (establishing the means to satisfy the constraint) and \textbf{defense} (protecting that means from the characteristic failure mode) yielding the six irreducible pillars.

A \emph{pillar} is a functionally distinct condition that must be materially or informationally instantiated for persistence. Pillars need not correspond one-to-one with components: a single mechanism may instantiate several pillars, and one pillar may be distributed across many mechanisms.

These pillars operate upon a medium that imposes immutable limits (bandwidth, latency, noise floor) referred to as the \textbf{Substrate}.

\subsection{The Six Pillars}
\label{subsec:pillars}

The following provide concrete realizations of the six pillars showing that each pillar has a convergent theorem from six unrelated fields.

\begin{enumerate}[label={}, leftmargin=0pt]
% Finiteness
    \item \textbf{Capacity ($CAP$):}
    The infrastructure required to acquire resources, process signals, and perform work. It defines the system's operational potential.
    \textit{Convergent Evidence (The Bekenstein Bound \cite{bekenstein_universal_1981}):} Bekenstein's bound limits the information content of any finite region, and the Margolus-Levitin limit\cite{margolus_maximum_1998} bounds the rate of state transitions; together they establish that finite capacity is a structural invariant.

    \item \textbf{Margin ($MAR$):}
    The structural and energetic reserves held to absorb stochastic shocks. It defines the system's `Safe Operating Area,' providing the buffer required to survive environmental variance without terminal failure. (The gain and phase margin).     
    \textit{Convergent Evidence (Nyquist-Bode Stability Margins\cite{nyquist_regeneration_1932} and Kingman's Formula\cite{kingman_single_1961}):} A system operating at theoretical criticality has zero tolerance for variance. Kingman's formula shows that as a queueing system's utilization approaches unity, waiting time diverges asymptotically, zero buffer against arrival variance means unbounded delay. Nyquist's stability margins are the linear-control manifestation of the same general requirement: persistent systems must maintain structural tolerance away from critical thresholds.

% Conservation
    \item \textbf{Map ($MAP$):}
    The retained organization, relational structure, or state criterion that distinguishes the system's viable configurations from its environment and from failed configurations. In adaptive systems, Map may be instantiated as an internal predictive model; in passive systems, it may be embodied directly in physical organization.
    \textit{Convergent Evidence (The Good-Regulator Theorem \cite{conant_every_1970}):} Conant and Ashby's Good-Regulator theorem establishes that persistent regulation requires an internal model; without Map, the system--environment boundary dissolves.

    \item \textbf{Protocol ($PRO$):}
    The communicative glue regulating information flow between pillars. It ensures internal coherence, standardizes interfaces, and minimizes signal decay across the system's internal boundaries. 
    \textit{Convergent Evidence (Shannon's Source-Channel Separation and Channel Capacity \cite{shannon_communication_1949}):} Shannon's coding results characterize conditions for reliable communication, providing a realization of the Protocol role without requiring a physically separate interface layer.

% Causality
    \item \textbf{Toll ($TOL$):}
    The finite temporal and resource requirements through which state transitions are physically or computationally realized. Toll need not be dissipative in every implementation; it records that ordered change must occur through a substrate with finite transition capabilities.
    \textit{Convergent Evidence (Landauer's Principle and Margolus-Levitin Limit \cite{landauer_irreversibility_1961,margolus_maximum_1998}):} On thermal substrates, irreversible operations (such as information erasure) carry a strict lower bound of $k_B T \ln 2$ (Landauer's Principle), but even broadly, execution latency (Margolus-Levitin) makes Toll a structural requirement. 

    \item \textbf{Governor ($GOV$):}
    The active or passive constraint structure that keeps evolution within the system's viable region by suppressing, redirecting, or terminating destabilizing transitions.
    \textit{Convergent Evidence (Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety \cite{ashby_introduction_1956}):} Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety requires that the constraint mechanism match the variety of perturbations; without Governor, runaway is unavoidable.
\end{enumerate}

With each pillar independently attested, the architecture becomes grounded with citable bounds and can be built on formally.

\subsection{Necessity: Counterfactual Failure Modes}
\label{subsec:pillars_necessity}
The necessity of the six pillars follows by analyzing the \textbf{Counterfactual Failure Mode} of a system lacking exactly one pillar. In every case, the system's viability collapses, limiting its expected lifetime $\tau$ to the onset of its characteristic perturbation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No Capacity (Starvation):} Without baseline operational infrastructure, the system cannot acquire, process, or export resources. Once existing reserves are spent, any perturbation is fatal; $\tau \to 0$ via rapid exhaustion.

    \item \textbf{No Margin (Fragility):} Operating at theoretical criticality leaves zero tolerance for environmental variance. Any non-zero perturbation exceeds the system's structural limits immediately, so the expected time to a fatal shock is finite; $\tau \to 0$ by random shock.

   \item \textbf{No Map (Dissolution):} Without an internal model, the system has no reference for coherence, and no boundary to maintain against its environment; $\tau \to 0$ as the system--environment boundary dissolves. 

    \item \textbf{No Protocol (Decoherence):} Components act on statistically independent, outdated states, with no mechanism to reconcile them; $\tau \to 0$ by organizational fragmentation.

    \item \textbf{No Toll (Zeno Collapse):} A causal sequence requires irreducibly discrete state transitions. Without an irreducible toll per operation, the sequence can be infinitely subdivided; no individual step remains distinguishable as a cause of the next, and the ordering required for well-defined evolution dissolves. $\tau \to 0$ by causal decoherence.
    
    \item \textbf{No Governor (Divergence):} Unconstrained positive feedback drives resource consumption toward unbounded growth, exhausting the substrate or diverging past any recoverable bound; $\tau \to 0$ by unbounded runaway.
\end{itemize}
    
Since the removal of any component results in $\tau \to 0$, every pillar is necessary.


\subsection{Completeness: Compositional Closure}
\label{subsec:pillars_sufficiency}
Proving sufficiency over an unbounded architectural space would require ruling out every possible construction, which no finite argument can do. Instead, every capability plausibly treated as an independent requirement has been found to decompose into the six pillars, with no counterexample identified to date.

Memory is Map content provisioned against temporal variance by Margin. Prediction is Map operation on incoming signal via Protocol. Repair is Governor-triggered re-provisioning within the Margin reserve. Replication is Protocol-mediated write of Map onto fresh Capacity. Advanced topologies such as feedforward networks, state observers, and adaptive controllers extend the six-pillar structure the same way: none introduces a new primitive, each is a composition within the existing six.

\subsection{The Cost of Persistence}
\label{subsec:cost_of_persistence}
A system pays continuously merely to remain distinguishable from its environment. The six pillars consume resources, acting as a continuous resistor against entropic erosion. This total structural cost provides the scalar metric required to compare systems.

Every pillar imposes a strictly positive cost, partitioning into two operational roles:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct Structural Costs:} The overhead of maintaining the system's baseline existence. Maintaining the vessel ($Z_{\mathrm{CAP}}$, $Z_{\mathrm{MAR}}$), the internal model ($Z_{\mathrm{MAP}}$), and executing state updates ($Z_{\mathrm{TOL}}$) requires a strict allocation of resources regardless of how the system is organized.

    \item \textbf{Coordination Costs:} Protocol ($Z_{\mathrm{PRO}}$) and Governor ($Z_{\mathrm{GOV}}$) incur direct structural costs to operate, but function as active constraints rather than passive structures. Their operational effect is to suppress uncoordinated variance. As in control theory, the active controller consumes continuous energy to operate, but its regulation ensures the closed loop's total dissipation remains strictly lower than the open-loop alternative.
\end{itemize}

To quantify baseline structural costs independently of environmental work, the system is evaluated at its zero task-flux limit. This defines the \emph{Quiescent Steady State} (QSS), a homeostatic ground state where internal housekeeping dissipation balances entropic decay. In this baseline, the six pillars operate along functionally uncoupled, orthogonal channels:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cost_of_persistence}
    \bar{Z}_{\mathrm{net}} = Z_{\mathrm{CAP}} + Z_{\mathrm{MAP}} + Z_{\mathrm{TOL}} + Z_{\mathrm{MAR}} + Z_{\mathrm{PRO}} + Z_{\mathrm{GOV}}
\end{equation}

Because the pillars occupy orthogonal domains at zero flux, any correlation between them is already priced into the individual coordination terms $Z_{\mathrm{PRO}}$ and $Z_{\mathrm{GOV}}$; no additional cross-term is needed to capture coupling between pillars within this baseline. Coupling outside QSS introduces non-linear contention, which we formalize in \cref{sec:open_systems} on open systems.

\subsection{Relation to Existing Disciplines}
\label{subsec:relations}
Existing fields studying systems that persist have encountered the six-pillar architecture already as they each are functional instantiations. In each case, the discipline addressed operational problems that sit downstream of these six pillars, taking the essential architecture as given.

\subsubsection{Relation to Information Theory}
\label{subsec:information_theory}
Information theory quantifies how representation, encoding, and noise constrain communication across physical mediums. In doing so, it relies on structural primitives that map directly to the six pillars: channel capacity (Capacity), codebook topologies (Map), framing and handshaking routines (Protocol), power constraints (Governor), processing overhead (Toll), and link margins (Margin). Information theory focuses on maximizing mutual information or minimizing error rates given a fixed architecture. Persistence Mechanics instead derives the architecture required for any state representation to survive noise and resist structural erasure.

\subsubsection{Relation to Thermodynamics}
\label{subsec:thermodynamics}
Thermodynamics describes how physical systems evolve under energy exchange and thermal fluctuations. It relies on physical primitives that correspond to the six pillars: extensive state variables and phase space volume (Capacity), metastable energy landscapes (Map), kinetic transition pathways (Protocol), conservation laws (Governor), irreversible entropy production (Toll), and free-energy activation barriers (Margin). Classical thermodynamics assumes the pillars, an external heat bath and continuous state variables, as given boundary conditions. Where thermodynamics describes how heat flows, persistence mechanics specifies what structural architecture must exist before heat, flow, or temperature can be meaningfully defined.

\subsubsection{Relation to Control Theory} 
\label{subsec:control_theory}
Classical control theory emerged from engineering physical servomechanisms: radar mounts, autopilots, and chemical plants whose historical context was optimizing systems already built, powered, and
staffed. Its four given components (the plant, the sensor, the controller, and the actuator) are convergent evidence for four of the six pillars: Plant/Actuator (Capacity), Reference Signal/Setpoint (Map), Sensor/Feedback Path (Protocol), Controller/Limiter (Governor). The remaining two pillars are rarely canonized alongside the first four as they are not physical components, but describe how the four components behave under time and stress. Time delays, bandwidth limits, and processing latency (Toll) were minimized as engineering overhead and gain/phase margins (Margin) were tuned for stability. Control theory asks how to regulate an existing system and the classical four-component loop is a physical instantiation of the six-pillar invariant.
