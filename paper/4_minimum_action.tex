\section{Operational Action and Survivorship}
\label{sec:minimum_action}

The Operational Action records what a history costs, but not which histories survive to be observed. The selection filter derives from the cost-dependent failures, and show that only minimal-action configurations remain observable.




\subsection{The Exponential Filter}
\label{subsec:exponential_filter}

A system exposed repeatedly to the possibility of terminal failure
must survive each exposure to remain observable. These successive
survival probabilities multiply. For example, if each update has
the same conditional probability $q\in(0,1)$ of avoiding terminal
failure, a history survives $N$ updates with probability $q^N$.
Here failure means leaving the specified viability region;
correctable local errors need not terminate persistence.

To connect this filter to Operational Action, fix a system boundary,
viability criterion, disturbance model, and observation interval
$[0,T]$. Let $\Gamma$ specify a candidate operational history,
conditional on which terminal failure remains uncertain. Let
$h_\Gamma(t)$ denote its terminal-failure hazard: the instantaneous
failure rate conditional on survival to time $t$.

We consider histories for which hazard is proportional to the
operational cost rate:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cost_proportional_hazard}
    h_\Gamma(t)
    = \beta Z_{\mathrm{net}}(t;\Gamma),
    \qquad \beta>0.
\end{equation}
The coefficient $\beta$ measures failure exposure per unit
operational cost. It is taken to be common to the compared
histories. This relation is a modeling assumption whose
applicability must be established for the systems considered.

For a locally integrable hazard, the probability of surviving
to time $T$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:survival_prob}
\begin{split}
    P(\tau>T\mid\Gamma)
    &= \exp\!\left[-\int_0^T h_\Gamma(t)\,dt\right] \\
    &= e^{-\beta S_\Phi[\Gamma;T]},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the failure time. Accumulated operational cost
therefore produces an exponential survival filter under the
stated relation.

The discrete-update example gives a concrete interpretation of
$\beta$. If every update costs $c_0>0$, then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:discrete_survival}
    S_\Phi=Nc_0,
    \qquad
    q^N=e^{-\beta S_\Phi},
    \qquad
    \beta=\frac{-\ln q}{c_0}.
\end{equation}
Survival is exponential in accumulated action. It is exponential
in elapsed time when the operational cost rate is constant.

\subsection{The Selection Principle}
\label{subsec:severity}

The filter becomes selective when histories accumulate different
operational costs. Compare a finite family
$\Gamma_1,\ldots,\Gamma_m$ under a common viability criterion,
observation interval, and resource accounting. Write
$S_i(T)=S_\Phi[\Gamma_i;T]$.

Let $p_i>0$, with $\sum_i p_i=1$, denote each candidate's initial
ensemble weight. These weights may represent initial frequencies
in a cohort or probabilities generated by a preparation or design
process. They describe representation before survival is assessed.

Conditioning on survival gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:survivor_distribution}
    \pi_i(T)
    \equiv P(\Gamma_i\mid\tau>T)
    =
    \frac{p_i e^{-\beta S_i(T)}}
         {\sum_j p_j e^{-\beta S_j(T)}}.
\end{equation}
The relative representation of two candidates is therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:severity}
    \frac{\pi_i(T)}{\pi_j(T)}
    =
    \frac{p_i}{p_j}
    e^{-\beta[S_i(T)-S_j(T)]}.
\end{equation}
Initial odds are multiplied by a survival penalty determined
by the action difference.

A recurring cost disadvantage can therefore overwhelm a large
initial advantage. A fixed initial cost difference, however,
produces only a fixed penalty if subsequent cost rates are
identical. Long-term concentration requires a difference that
continues to accumulate.

\begin{mdframed}[
    linewidth=1pt,
    linecolor=black,
    backgroundcolor=gray!10
]
\noindent\textbf{Selection Principle.}
Within a finite candidate family with fixed positive initial
weights and a common cost-proportional hazard, histories with
the lowest asymptotic operational cost rate dominate the
distribution conditional on survival as the observation
interval grows.
\end{mdframed}

This principle concerns the composition of the surviving ensemble.
It does not make higher-cost histories impossible at finite times
or guarantee that any candidate survives indefinitely. The model
follows differential survival; reproduction, immigration, or
transitions between candidate types require additional dynamics.

\subsection{Selection Without Foresight}
\label{subsec:teleology}

No system needs to evaluate alternatives or anticipate its future.
Differential failure changes which histories remain represented,
so the surviving ensemble can resemble the outcome of optimization
without an optimizing agent. This preference is relative to the
available candidates and does not imply global optimality or
certain survival.

\subsection{Formal Statement and Selection Timescale}
\label{subsec:minimum_action_as_survivorship}

The following proposition makes the long-term statement precise.

\begin{proposition}[Selection by Operational Action]
Consider a finite candidate family with fixed positive initial
weights and a common coefficient $\beta>0$ satisfying
\cref{eq:cost_proportional_hazard}. Suppose
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:asymptotic_action}
    S_i(T)=s_iT+o(T),
\end{equation}
where $s_i$ is the asymptotic operational cost rate. Let
\[
    s_*=\min_j s_j,
    \qquad
    I_*=\{i:s_i=s_*\}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:selection_principle}
    \lim_{T\to\infty}\sum_{i\in I_*}\pi_i(T)=1.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Choose a candidate $\Gamma_*$ with asymptotic rate $s_*$.
For any candidate with $s_i>s_*$,
\[
    \frac{\pi_i(T)}{\pi_*(T)}
    =
    \frac{p_i}{p_*}
    \exp\!\left[-\beta\bigl((s_i-s_*)T+o(T)\bigr)\right]
    \longrightarrow0.
\]
Since $\pi_*(T)\leq1$, it follows that $\pi_i(T)\to0$.
There are finitely many higher-rate candidates, so their
total conditional probability tends to zero.
\end{proof}

For constant cost rates with gap $\Delta s=s_i-s_*>0$,
the relative survivor odds decrease by a factor of $e$
over the characteristic time
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:selection_timescale}
    \tau_{\mathrm{select}}
    =\frac{1}{\beta\Delta s}.
\end{equation}
Intervals short compared with this timescale produce little
reweighting of the initial odds. Over many such intervals,
a persistent disadvantage produces strong relative suppression.
For asymptotically constant rates, this expression gives the
leading long-time exponential scale rather than an exact
finite-time prediction.

\subsection{Scope: Protective Architecture}
\label{subsec:protective_architecture}

Margin and Governor can increase expenditure while reducing
failure exposure. If the cost--hazard coefficient differs
across architectures or through time, survival is governed by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cumulative_hazard}
    H_i(T)=\int_0^T
    \beta_i(t)Z_{\mathrm{net}}(t;\Gamma_i)\,dt,
\end{equation}
with survivor weights proportional to $p_i e^{-H_i(T)}$.
The Operational Action result follows when all candidates
share a constant $\beta$; otherwise, protective expenditure
can favor a higher-cost architecture. Applying the common-
coefficient model requires testing whether negative log
survival is proportional to accumulated cost with the same
coefficient across candidates.

\subsection{Relation to Least-Action Principles}
\label{subsec:relation_to_least_action}

At a fixed observation time, the most probable surviving candidates minimize
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:effective_selection_cost}
    \mathcal{I}_i(T)=\beta S_i(T)-\ln p_i.
\end{equation}
For equal initial weights, this reduces to minimizing Operational Action within the candidate family.

This resembles a least-action principle, but mechanical action integrates a Lagrangian and determines stationary trajectories under specified variations
\cite[Vol.~II, Chap.~19]{feynman_lectures_1964}.
Operational Action accumulates resource cost. Establishing a physical equivalence requires a correspondence between the functionals, their units, and their admissible histories.

\section{Minimum Action as Survivorship}
\label{sec:minimum_action}
The Operational Action defines what a system must minimize, but not which histories survive to be observed. The selection filter derives from the multiplicative compounding of independent transition risk, and show that only minimal-action configurations remain observable.


\subsection{Operational Action}
\label{subsec:operational_action}
% First we need to add across the system, adding up all 6 pillars, Each component or location has a cost rate. Adding them gives the whole system’s cost rate at time \(t\). For discrete components, the integral is simply a sum.

While the operational cost rate describes instantaneous expenditure, evaluating the accumulated burden of distinct histories requires integrating this rate across the system's spatial extent and temporal duration.

Let $\Sigma(t)$ denote the physical or computational elements defining the system boundary at time $t$. Integrating the local cost-rate density over this footprint yields the total instantaneous operational cost:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:impedance_density}
    Z_{\mathrm{net}}(t)
    = \int_{\Sigma(t)} \mathcal{D}(x,t)\,d\mu(x).
\end{equation}
Here, $\mu$ specifies the substrate measure used to define $\mathcal{D}$. For discrete elements, the counting measure reduces this integral to a sum over the localized elements.

The architectural pillars need not occupy physically disjoint regions of the substrate. Because the six pillars represent distinct functional requirements, their total expenditure partitions exactly into their additive costs (\cref{subsec:cost_of_persistence}), yielding $Z_{\mathrm{net}}(t)=\sum_i Z_i(t)$. This strict accounting ensures that even if a single physical mechanism multiplexes multiple pillars, its operational cost is uniquely apportioned without double-counting.

% Accumulate through time.
The \emph{Operational Action} $S_{\Phi}$ of a structural history $\Gamma$ over an observation interval $[0,T]$ is defined as its cumulative operational cost:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\label{eq:operational_action}
    S_{\Phi}[\Gamma;T]
    &= \int_0^T Z_{\mathrm{net}}(t;\Gamma)\,dt \\
    &= \int_0^T\int_{\Sigma(t)} 
      \mathcal{D}(x,t;\Gamma)\,d\mu(x)\,dt.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Comparing competing systems requires evaluating this action over a common observation interval, a shared resource unit, and a consistent convention for identifying the system boundary.

The dimensions of $S_{\Phi}$ inherit the units of the operational toll $c$. If $c$ represents dissipated energy per operation, $S_{\Phi}$ quantifies macroscopic dissipated energy. If $c$ is defined as a dimensionless action per operation, $S_{\Phi}$ becomes a pure, dimensionless count of state transitions. In arbitrary physical systems, a dimensionless Operational Action can always be recovered by dividing $S_{\Phi}$ by a characteristic reference cost.

While the static architecture identifies the functions instantiated by the system, the Operational Action records the exact thermodynamic burden of their realized operation. This cost accumulates along any history, whether or not the system ultimately persists. Relating this accumulated Operational Action to the probability of survival requires a specific failure model, formalized in \cref{sec:minimum_action}.


\subsection{The Exponential Filter}
\label{subsec:exponential_filter}
If persistence is maintained by state transitions, and each transition risks scrambling information into the substrate noise floor, then a history of $N$ transitions survives only if every step survives. Independent errors compound multiplicatively, yielding an exponential filter.

Consider a structural history $\Gamma$ accumulating operational action $S_\Phi[\Gamma]$ (\cref{eq:operational_action}). Because the substrate possesses a finite noise floor, each elementary transition carries an intrinsic risk of information loss. Let $q$ be the probability that a single elementary transition maintains causal coherence.

In QSS, successive transitions are statistically independent dissipative events. The probability that $\Gamma$ maintains coherence is therefore multiplicative:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:survival_prob1}
    P(\text{survival} \mid \Gamma) 
    = q^{S_\Phi[\Gamma]} 
\end{equation}

Using the identity $q^C = e^{C\ln q}$ with $C = S_\Phi[\Gamma]$, and defining $\beta \equiv -\ln q > 0$ (positive since $0 < q < 1$), we have $S_\Phi[\Gamma]\ln q = -\beta S_\Phi[\Gamma]$, yielding the survival probability distribution:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:survival_prob}
    P(\text{survival} \mid \Gamma) = e^{-\beta S_\Phi[\Gamma]}
\end{equation}

Here $\beta \equiv -\ln q$ is the substrate's inverse operational tolerance, a geometric invariant of the channel. For discrete substrates, $q$ is determined by the channel geometry; in continuous media, $\beta$ emerges from the substrate's noise spectral density.

This is the informational analogue of the Boltzmann distribution $P \propto e^{-E/k_BT}$, with $S_\Phi$ as energy and $\beta$ as inverse temperature. The Boltzmann form requires ensemble equilibrium and a thermal bath; the present form requires only independent transition risk along a single history and a substrate noise floor. The informational route recovers the same exponential law under strictly weaker assumptions: substrate geometry replaces thermodynamic equilibrium.


\subsection{The Severity of the Exponential Filter}
\label{subsec:severity}

The operational severity of this filter can be quantified by comparing trajectories. Let $\Gamma_{\text{min}}$ denote the minimal-action trajectory and $\Gamma_{\text{var}}$ any non-minimal path with an action deviation $\delta S_\Phi > 0$. Over any observation window, the relative survival probability is:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:severity}
    \frac{P(\Gamma_{\text{var}})}{P(\Gamma_{\text{min}})} = e^{-\beta \delta S_\Phi}
\end{equation}

When the fundamental update cycle is much shorter than the observation timescale, even a small per-transition deviation compounds into a vast accumulated $\delta S_\Phi$, and the survival ratio $P(\Gamma_{\text{var}})/P(\Gamma_{\text{min}})$ falls to zero. The gap between the best trajectory and the next-best widens without bound.

Because survival probability depends exponentially on $S_\Phi$, only histories approaching the minimum retain non-negligible observation probability:

\begin{samepage}
\begin{mdframed}[linewidth=1pt,linecolor=black,backgroundcolor=gray!10]
    \noindent The \textbf{Selection Principle}: Among all configurations capable of encoding a given structural complexity, only those possessing the minimal possible Operational Action ($S_\Phi$) asymptotically dominate the observable distribution.
\end{mdframed}
\end{samepage}

Because $S_\Phi$ counts elementary transitions, it is the cumulative ledger of a system's dissipation history. The substrate does not optimize this ledger; it erases ledgers that fail to balance.

\subsection{Eliminative vs. Teleological Selection}
\label{subsec:teleology}
The severity of the exponential filter is a statement about the losers, not the winner. It says nothing about whether $\Gamma_{\text{min}}$ is efficient, elegant, or well-suited to anything, only that every alternative to it decayed faster. A sub-optimal trajectory survives just as certainly as an optimal one, provided everything else was worse. What looks like design, foresight, or purpose is not a property of the winner; it is the absence of its competition.

\subsection{Minimum Action as Survivorship}
\label{subsec:minimum_action_as_survivorship}
This eliminative mechanism is formalized as:

\begin{proposition}[Minimum Action as Survivorship]
In QSS, the observable trajectories of any persistent system are those achieving the minimal Operational Action $S_\Phi$ among all candidate trajectories.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{eq:survival_prob}, the survival probability of any history $\Gamma$ is $P(\text{survival} \mid \Gamma) = e^{-\beta S_\Phi[\Gamma]}$. For any non-minimal trajectory $\Gamma_{\text{var}}$ with $\delta S_\Phi = S_\Phi[\Gamma_{\text{var}}] - S_\Phi[\Gamma_{\text{min}}] > 0$, \cref{eq:severity} gives $P(\Gamma_{\text{var}})/P(\Gamma_{\text{min}}) = e^{-\beta \delta S_\Phi}$. As $\delta S_\Phi$ accumulates over the observation window, this ratio vanishes. Therefore, only trajectories achieving the minimal Operational Action retain non-negligible observation probability. The observable set is the argmin of $S_\Phi[\Gamma]$.
\end{proof}

Standard quantum mechanics grounds the Principle of Least Action in phase interference. The Selection Principle offers a distinct, classical mechanism (informational elimination) by which candidate physical histories are selected: the substrate itself acts as a filter, and what remains observable is only what has not yet dissipated.
